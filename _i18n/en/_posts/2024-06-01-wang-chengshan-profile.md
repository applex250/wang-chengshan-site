> Researchers need the spirit of scaling new heights and daring to be the first — only by focusing on one thing and digging deeper and deeper can one encounter the most beautiful sea of stars along the journey.

Wang Chengshan (November 1951–), from Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, is a geologist, sedimentologist and academician of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. In 1977, he graduated with a bachelor's degree from the Department of Ore Prospecting of Chengdu College of Geology (now Chengdu University of Technology); in 1981, he received a master's degree in geology from the same college. He has served as Director of the Institute of Sedimentary Geology at Chengdu College of Geology and President of Chengdu University of Technology. His research focuses mainly on sedimentology and tectonics, with special emphasis on the uplift of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and deep-time palaeoclimate and palaeoceanography. He has served as Chief Scientist of two national "973" projects and leader of two UNESCO International Geoscience Programme (IGCP) projects, and as Chief Scientist he led the completion of the 20-year ICDP project — the "International Continental Scientific Drilling Project of the Songliao Basin".

## The Starting Point of a Scientific Career

In 1978, in Changdu, Tibet. A young technician named Wang Chengshan, carrying a heavy load of rock specimens, trudged with difficulty across the gravel terraces around Yulong Snow Mountain on the plateau.

The sun scorched his skin and wind-blown sand choked his throat. He paid them no mind, focusing all his attention on the bare rock strata beneath his feet.

This plateau, hailed as a "natural geological laboratory", was the starting point of his scientific career.

## From Fundamental Geological Research to the Frontiers of Astronomy

In 2024, a paper revealing that Earth's rotation undergoes a stepwise deceleration was published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* of the United States. The study was led by the Big Data Sedimentology team of Chengdu University of Technology, in collaboration with several world-leading institutions including the Paris Observatory (France), the Leibniz Institute for Applied Geophysics (Germany) and Trinity College Dublin (Ireland), further revealing the subtle yet profound intrinsic link between Earth's rotational rhythms and surface environmental change.

The supervisor behind this research achievement was none other than Wang Chengshan.

Nearly half a century later, the young man who had once hammered rocks and questioned the Earth on the plateau completed the leap from fundamental geological research to the frontiers of astronomy.
